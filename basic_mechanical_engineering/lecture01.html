Lecture 1 · Statics

Statics of a particle and of a rigid body

Engineering design starts with one question: what forces act on this part, and is it in balance? Today we build the tools to answer it. These are vectors, components, moments and couples, and the equilibrium equations.

After this lecture you can…

  • resolve a force into rectangular components and add forces into a resultant;
  • write equilibrium conditions for a particle and solve for unknown forces;
  • compute the moment of a force about a point (and use Varignon’s theorem);
  • replace a force by a force–couple system, draw a free-body diagram and find support reactions.

Syllabus outcomes: 1MEC1.1, 1MEC3.2, 1MEC6.1 · Reading: Beer & Johnston, Vector Mechanics for Engineers – Statics, ch. 2–4; Meriam & Kraige vol. 1, ch. 2–3.

Scalars, vectors and forces

A scalar has only a magnitude (mass, time, temperature). A vector has a magnitude and a direction and adds by the parallelogram law. A force is a vector with a point of application. For a rigid body only its line of action matters (the principle of transmissibility).

In 2D we describe a force F by its rectangular components:

Fx = F cos θ,    Fy = F sin θ,    F = √(Fx² + Fy²),    tan θ = Fy / Fx

The resultant of several concurrent forces is found by adding components: Rx = ΣFx, Ry = ΣFy. Graphical (parallelogram, polygon) and analytical methods always give the same answer. Try it below.

Equilibrium of a particle

A particle is in equilibrium when the resultant of all forces acting on it is zero (Newton’s first law):

R = ΣF = 0   ⇔   ΣFx = 0,   ΣFy = 0two scalar equations → at most two unknowns in 2D

Graphically, the forces form a closed polygon. For three forces this is a force triangle. Procedure: (1) isolate the particle, (2) draw its free-body diagram (FBD), (3) write ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0, (4) solve.

Moment of a force and Varignon’s theorem

A force tends to rotate a body about a point. This turning effect is the moment:

MO = r × F,    |MO| = F · d = r F sin θ d = perpendicular distance from O to the line of action · unit N·m · counter-clockwise (+)

Varignon’s theorem: the moment of a force about a point equals the sum of the moments of its components: MO = x Fy − y Fx. In practice this is usually the fastest way to compute a moment.

Couples and force–couple systems

Two equal, opposite, non-collinear forces form a couple. Its moment M = F d is the same about every point, so a couple is a free vector. Consequence: any force F acting at A can be moved to any point O if we add a couple MO = r × F. Tick “equivalent force–couple at O” above to see it. A whole system of forces can be reduced the same way to one resultant force R = ΣF and one resultant moment MOR = ΣMO.

Physical demo idea: bring a real wrench. Ask why pushing at 90° works best, and why a longer wrench (“cheater bar”) helps.

Equilibrium of a rigid body

A rigid body is in equilibrium when the force system acting on it reduces to zero force and zero moment:

ΣFx = 0,   ΣFy = 0,   ΣMA = 0three independent equations in 2D (A is any point – choose one where unknowns meet)

Free-body diagram, step by step

  1. Cut the body free from its supports.
  2. Draw all applied loads (forces, couples, weight).
  3. Replace each support by its reactions (assume a sense).
  4. Add dimensions, then write the equations.
  5. A negative result means the assumed sense was wrong.

Key misconception: students “move” the couple and expect the reactions to change. Let them drag the couple slider. This is the free-vector property in action.

Quick check

1. A force is slid along its own line of action. Its moment about point O…

The perpendicular distance d from O to the line of action is unchanged (principle of transmissibility), so M = F·d is unchanged.

2. A weight W hangs from two cables, each at 30° to the horizontal. The tension in each cable is…

2T sin 30° = W ⇒ T = W. Check it in the cable demo.

3. How many unknown reactions does a fixed (clamped) support have in 2D?

Two force components and one reaction moment. It prevents both translations and rotation.

Summary

  • Forces are vectors: add them by components, Rx = ΣFx, Ry = ΣFy.
  • Particle in equilibrium: ΣF = 0 (2 equations in 2D). The force polygon closes.
  • Moment MO = F d. Varignon: sum the moments of the components.
  • A couple is a free vector. A force can be moved to another point if you add a couple.
  • Rigid body in equilibrium: ΣFx = ΣFy = ΣM = 0. Always start from a free-body diagram.
