Lecture 2 · Statics

Distributed forces, centroids, structures and friction

Real loads are rarely single arrows. Snow, water pressure and a body’s own weight are spread out. Today we replace distributed loads by equivalent resultants, locate centroids, analyse trusses joint by joint, and meet friction, the force that holds the world together.

After this lecture you can…

  • replace a distributed load by its resultant and find where it acts;
  • compute the centroid of composite areas and use the Pappus–Guldinus theorems;
  • find member forces in a plane truss (method of joints / sections) and recognise zero-force members;
  • apply the Coulomb friction model: decide whether a body slips and compute the friction force.

Distributed loads and their resultant

A load spread along a beam is described by its intensity q(x) [N/m]. For equilibrium we may replace it with a single force equal to the area under the load curve, acting through the centroid of that area:

W = ∫ q(x) dx,    x̄ = ∫ x q(x) dx / W uniform load: W = qL at L/2 · triangular load: W = ½ q₀L at L/3 from the larger end

The equivalent resultant is valid for external equilibrium (reactions) only. Internal forces and deformation (Lectures 7–10) depend on how the load is really distributed.

Centre of gravity, centre of mass, centroid

The centre of gravity G is the point where the total weight acts. For a homogeneous body it coincides with the centre of mass and the geometric centroid. For lines, areas and volumes:

x̄ = ∫ x dA / A,   ȳ = ∫ y dA / A   →   composite:   x̄ = ΣAi x̄i / ΣAi,   ȳ = ΣAi ȳi / ΣAi The products Qx = Σ Aiȳi, Qy = Σ Aix̄i are first moments of area. A hole counts as a negative area.

An axis of symmetry always contains the centroid. Use it to save work.

Theorems of Pappus–Guldinus

Rotating a plane curve or area about a non-intersecting axis generates a surface or a body of revolution. Its size follows directly from the distance travelled by the centroid:

Surface:   A = 2π ȳL L     Volume:   V = 2π ȳA A ȳ = distance of the centroid of the generating curve (length L) or area (A) from the axis

Statics of structures: trusses, frames, machines

A truss is made of straight two-force members joined by pins at their ends and loaded only at the joints. Each member carries an axial force: tension (pulls on the joint) or compression (pushes). A plane truss with j joints and m members on 3 reaction components is statically determinate when

m + 3 = 2j

Method of joints

Isolate each joint (a particle). Write ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0. Start where only two forces are unknown. Assume tension; a negative answer means compression.

Method of sections

Cut through at most three members. Take moments about the point where two of the cut members meet, which gives the third directly. This is fast when you need a single member force.

Zero-force members. (1) If an unloaded joint has two non-collinear members, both are zero. (2) If an unloaded joint has three members and two are collinear, the third is zero. These members are not useless: they brace the structure against buckling and against other load cases. Move the load in the demo and watch them switch.

Frames and machines contain at least one multi-force member (e.g. pliers, an excavator arm). Dismember them, draw an FBD of each part, and use action = reaction at the connecting pins.

Increase the truss height and show that chord forces drop roughly as 1/h. This is the same idea as the lever arm of internal moments in bending (Lecture 9).

Friction

Dry (Coulomb) friction acts tangentially at the contact and opposes impending or actual sliding. Up to the limit it is whatever is needed for equilibrium:

F ≤ μs N (no slip),    F = μk N (sliding),    tan φs = μs μs – static, μk < μs – kinetic coefficient · φs – angle of friction (on an incline: angle of repose)

Other friction models

  • Viscous (lubricated): F = c v.
  • Rolling resistance: F = (a / r) N, with a the deformation lever arm.
  • Belt friction (Euler–Eytelwein): T₂ / T₁ = eμβ, with β the wrap angle in rad.

Quick check

1. A triangular load rises from 0 to q₀ over length L. Its resultant acts…

The centroid of a triangle is at one-third of its base from the tall side, so it is 2L/3 from the zero end. The resultant is ½q₀L.

2. A block rests on an incline at θ = 10°, μs = 0.5. The friction force equals…

Static friction is only as large as equilibrium requires. μsN is the maximum, and it is reached only when slip is impending.

3. An unloaded truss joint connects exactly two non-collinear members. Their forces are…

The two directions are independent. ΣF = 0 forces each component, and therefore each member force, to vanish.

Summary

  • Distributed load → resultant = area under q(x), acting at its centroid.
  • Composite centroid: x̄ = ΣAix̄i/ΣAi. Holes are negative areas.
  • Pappus–Guldinus: A = 2π ȳ L, V = 2π ȳ A.
  • Trusses: two-force members, m + 3 = 2j, joints or sections, zero-force members.
  • Friction: F ≤ μsN at rest, F = μkN when sliding.
