Lecture 5 · Dynamics

Dynamics of a particle: Newton’s laws and momentum

Kinematics told us how things move. Kinetics tells us why. One equation, ΣF = m a, and its integrated forms (impulse–momentum, angular momentum) cover a huge range of engineering problems: braking distances, crash safety and spinning machinery.

After this lecture you can…

  • state Newton’s laws and write the equation of motion of a particle in rectangular and n–t components;
  • solve connected-body problems (pulleys, inclines, friction);
  • apply the impulse–momentum principle and conservation of linear momentum (impacts, coefficient of restitution);
  • use angular momentum HO = r × mv and its conservation under central forces.

Syllabus outcomes: 1MEC1.1, 1MEC3.2 · Reading: Beer & Johnston – Dynamics, ch. 12–13; Meriam & Kraige vol. 2, ch. 3. Related lab: Test 5 (conservation of momentum).

Newton’s laws and the equation of motion

  1. First law: with zero resultant force, a particle stays at rest or moves with constant velocity.
  2. Second law: ΣF = m a, valid in an inertial (non-accelerating) frame.
  3. Third law: action = reaction. Equal, opposite and collinear forces between two bodies.
Recipe for kinetics problems:
1. Free-body diagram (forces)
2. Kinetic diagram (ma)
3. ΣFx = m ax, ΣFy = m ay (or n–t)
4. Kinematic constraints (ropes, contact)
5. Solve and check units and signs

Normal and tangential components: vehicle on a curve

In path coordinates the second law reads ΣFt = m dv/dt, ΣFn = m v²/ρ. A curve needs an inward force m v²/ρ. It is supplied by friction, by banking (the inclined normal force), or by both.

Linear momentum and impulse

Newton actually wrote the second law as a rate of change of linear momentum p = mv. Integrating over time gives the impulse–momentum principle:

ΣF = dp/dt   ⇒   mv₁ + ∫t₁t₂ ΣF dt = mv₂ impulse = area under F(t) [N·s]. The same impulse spread over a longer time needs a smaller peak force.

Conservation of momentum and impact

If no external impulse acts on a system (internal forces cancel by Newton’s third law), its total momentum is conserved. In a collision the contact forces are internal, so:

m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁′ + m₂v₂′,    e = (v₂′ − v₁′) / (v₁ − v₂) e = 1 perfectly elastic (KE conserved) · 0 < e < 1 real impacts · e = 0 perfectly plastic (bodies stick together)

This is exactly Lab Test 5 (air track). Ask: m₁ = m₂, e = 1, v₂ = 0. What happens? The carts exchange velocities (Newton’s cradle).

Angular momentum of a particle

The moment of linear momentum about a point O is the angular momentum. Its rate of change equals the moment of the forces:

HO = r × mv,   |HO| = m v r sin φ,    ΣMO = dHO/dt central force (always through O) ⇒ ΣMO = 0 ⇒ HO = const ⇒ r · vθ = const

The same law explains why a figure skater spins faster when pulling the arms in, and Kepler’s second law (equal areas in equal times). Kinetic energy is not conserved here: the hand pulling the string does work.

Quick check

1. Mass m₂ hangs on a rope over a pulley and accelerates downward. The rope tension is…

m₂g − T = m₂a with a > 0 ⇒ T < m₂g.

2. Two carts collide and stick together (e = 0). Which quantity is conserved?

Momentum is conserved for any internal impact. Kinetic energy is lost to deformation and heat.

3. The string radius of the spinning puck is halved. Its speed…

H = m v r = const ⇒ v ∝ 1/r. The string tension m v²/r grows 8×.

Summary

  • ΣF = m a in an inertial frame. Draw the FBD and the kinetic diagram.
  • n–t form: ΣFn = m v²/ρ. Curves need an inward force.
  • Impulse–momentum: m v₁ + ∫F dt = m v₂. A longer impact time means a smaller force.
  • No external impulse ⇒ momentum conserved. Coefficient of restitution e.
  • HO = r × mv, ΣMO = dHO/dt. Central force ⇒ HO conserved.
