Plane motion of a rigid body

A rigid body’s points do not all move the same way. The tip of a crank, the rim of a wheel and the piston pin all follow different paths. Yet one angular velocity ω and one reference point describe them all. Today we learn to read velocities straight off a mechanism with the instantaneous centre of rotation.

After this lecture you can…

  • classify motion as translation, rotation about a fixed axis or general plane motion;
  • compute velocity and acceleration of points on a rotating body (v = ωr, at = εr, an = ω²r);
  • use vB = vA + ω × rB/A for general plane motion;
  • locate the instantaneous centre of rotation (ICR) and use it to find velocities in a mechanism.

Syllabus outcomes: 1MEC1.1, 1MEC3.2 · Reading: Beer & Johnston – Dynamics, ch. 15; Meriam & Kraige vol. 2, ch. 5.

Three types of plane motion

  • Translation: every line in the body keeps its direction. All points have the same velocity and acceleration. Paths may be straight or curved (curvilinear translation).
  • Rotation about a fixed axis: points move on circles about the axis.
  • General plane motion: any other planar motion. It is always a combination of translation + rotation.
Angular velocity is a property of the whole body. Every line drawn on a rigid body rotates by the same angle in the same time, so ω and ε are the same for every point. Only v and a differ from point to point.

Curvilinear translation surprises students: the Ferris-wheel gondola or the parallel-link plate moves on a circle but does not rotate (ω = 0). The ladder (general motion) midpoint traces a circle, which makes a nice exam question.

Gears and belts. Meshing gears (or a non-slipping belt) share the same contact-point speed: ω₁ r₁ = ω₂ r₂, hence the transmission ratio i = ω₁/ω₂ = r₂/r₁.

General plane motion: relative velocity

Pick any point A of the body as a reference. Relative to A the body only rotates, so:

vB = vA + ω × rB/A,    |ω × rB/A| = ω · AB,   direction ⊥ AB accelerations: aB = aA + ε × rB/A − ω² rB/A
Useful corollary: the velocity components of A and B along the line AB are equal, because a rigid body cannot stretch. This is often the quickest check of a velocity diagram.

Instantaneous centre of rotation (ICR)

At any instant there is a point I (possibly outside the body) with zero velocity. At that instant the body moves as if it were rotating about I:

vP = ω · |IP|,   vP ⊥ IP How to find I: draw perpendiculars to the velocities of two points and intersect them. Parallel perpendiculars mean I is at infinity, so the body is in instantaneous translation.

The ICR has zero velocity, but in general not zero acceleration. The contact point of a rolling wheel accelerates upward with ω²R. Never use the ICR for accelerations.

Application: crank–slider mechanism

This is the heart of every piston engine and compressor. The crank OA rotates about O. The connecting rod AB is in general plane motion, and the piston B translates. The rod’s ICR is where the line OA (⊥ vA) meets the vertical through B (⊥ vB).

Quick check

1. A car moves at 72 km/h on wheels of radius 0.3 m (no slip). The top of the tyre moves at…

The ICR is at the contact point. The top is at distance 2R, so v = ω·2R = 2vC = 40 m/s.

2. A gondola on a Ferris wheel stays upright. Its motion is…

Lines on the gondola keep their direction (ω = 0), even though its points move on circles.

3. The ICR of a body is at infinity. Then…

ω = 0 at that instant (instantaneous translation). Accelerations can still differ.

Summary

  • Translation: all points move identically. Rotation: v = ωr, at = εr, an = ω²r.
  • General plane motion = translation of a reference point + rotation: vB = vA + ω × rB/A.
  • ICR: intersect the perpendiculars to two velocities; then v = ω·(distance from I).
  • Rolling without slipping: the contact point is the ICR, vC = ωR.
