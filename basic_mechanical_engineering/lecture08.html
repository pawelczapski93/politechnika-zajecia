Lecture 8 · Mechanics of materials

Shear, joints and stress analysis

Bolts, rivets, pins, welds and keys are loaded mainly in shear. Also, a stress that is “pure tension” in one direction looks like tension and shear on an inclined plane. Today we learn to rotate the stress state with Mohr’s circle and to link stresses to strains in 3D.

After this lecture you can…

  • define shear stress and shear strain and use Hooke’s law for shear, τ = Gγ;
  • size bolted and pinned joints for shear, bearing and net-section tension;
  • transform plane stress, find principal stresses and maximum shear with Mohr’s circle;
  • apply the generalised (3D) Hooke’s law and compute volumetric strain.

Shear stress, pure shear and Hooke’s law for shear

A force acting parallel to a surface produces shear stress τ. Moment equilibrium of a small element requires complementary shear stresses: equal τ on perpendicular faces, pointing toward or away from the same corner. The element distorts by the shear strain γ, a change of the right angle measured in radians.

τavg = V / A,    τ = G γ,    G = E / [2(1 + ν)] steel: G ≈ 81 GPa · aluminium: G ≈ 26 GPa · E, ν, G are not independent for isotropic materials

Shear stresses in joints

A bolted (or riveted, pinned) joint can fail in three ways:

  1. Shear of the bolt: τ = F / (n · m · πd²/4), where m = number of shear planes (1 single, 2 double shear).
  2. Bearing (crushing) of hole/bolt: σb = F / (n · d · t).
  3. Tension of the plate in the net section: σ = F / [(b − nrowd) t].
Design condition: every stress ≤ its allowable value (kt, kb, kr). The joint capacity is the smallest of the three. Double shear doubles bolt shear capacity and removes the bending eccentricity of a lap joint.

Plane stress transformation and Mohr’s circle

The same stress state looks different on differently oriented planes. Rotating the element by θ (counter-clockwise):

σx′ = (σx+σy)/2 + (σx−σy)/2 · cos 2θ + τxy sin 2θ,    τx′y′ = −(σx−σy)/2 · sin 2θ + τxy cos 2θ principal stresses σ₁,₂ = C ± R, C = (σx+σy)/2, R = √[((σx−σy)/2)² + τxy²], tan 2θp = 2τxy/(σx−σy), τmax = R

Convention used here: τ is plotted positive downward. Then a counter-clockwise rotation θ of the element is a counter-clockwise rotation 2θ on the circle. Principal planes carry no shear stress. Maximum shear planes lie at 45° to them.

Generalised Hooke’s law

Pull a bar and it gets thinner (Poisson). In 3D, every normal stress causes strain in all three directions. Shear stresses only cause shear strains (isotropic material):

εx = [σx − ν(σy + σz)] / E   (and cyclically),    γxy = τxy / G volumetric strain e = ΔV/V = εx + εy + εz = (1 − 2ν)(σx + σy + σz)/E · ν = 0.5 ⇒ incompressible

Quick check

1. A pin in double shear carries F. The shear stress in the pin is…

Two shear planes share the load, each carrying F/2.

2. Uniaxial tension σ. The maximum shear stress is…

The Mohr’s circle passes through (σ, 0) and (0, 0). Its radius is σ/2. This is why ductile metals yield along 45° slip bands.

3. Steel has E = 210 GPa, ν = 0.3. G equals…

G = E/[2(1+ν)] = 210/2.6 ≈ 80.8 GPa. Lab Test 11 measures G in torsion.

Summary

  • τ = V/A, τ = Gγ, G = E/[2(1+ν)]. Shear stresses come in complementary pairs.
  • Joints: check bolt shear (count the shear planes), bearing and net-section tension.
  • Stress transformation: Mohr’s circle, centre C, radius R. σ₁,₂ = C ± R, τmax = R.
  • 3D Hooke: εx = [σx − ν(σy+σz)]/E. Volumetric strain ∝ (1−2ν).
